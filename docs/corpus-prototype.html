Joint Pruning-Aware Quantization Across Model Scales

R. Adeyemi · L. Nakashima · P. Sørensen

Institute for Efficient Computation

Abstract

Compressing large language models typically proceeds in two stages: unstructured pruning followed by post-training quantization. We ask whether performing these steps jointly — optimizing a single objective aware of both the sparsity mask and the quantization grid — outperforms applying them in sequence. Across the Pythia suite from 70M to 6.9B parameters, we find that joint compression yields a consistent advantage at small scale, but that the advantage narrows and then reverses as model size grows. We attribute this to the increasing redundancy of large models, which makes the sequential approximation error less costly to absorb.

1Introduction

Deployment cost, not training cost, dominates the lifetime expense of a served language model. Pruning and quantization are the two dominant levers for reducing it, and both are well characterised in isolation. Practitioners almost universally apply them sequentially, largely for engineering convenience: pruning tooling and quantization tooling are maintained separately. Whether that convenience carries an accuracy cost, and whether any such cost is uniform across model scale, has not been measured systematically.

We make three contributions. First, we formalise a joint objective that treats the sparsity mask and the quantization scale as a single optimization problem. Second, we evaluate it against a strong sequential baseline at seven model sizes spanning two orders of magnitude. Third, we characterise a crossover point beyond which joint optimization no longer pays for its additional complexity.

1

2Background

Magnitude pruning removes weights whose absolute value falls below a layer-wise threshold. Post-training quantization maps the surviving weights onto a lower-bit grid, typically by minimising per-channel reconstruction error against a small calibration set. Applied in sequence, the second stage inherits whatever error the first introduced and cannot revisit it.

3Method

3.1Joint objective

We define a single objective in which the sparsity mask M and the quantization scale s are optimized together against a calibration set, rather than fixing M before s is chosen. Because the mask is discrete, we relax it during optimization using a straight-through estimator and anneal the temperature over 500 steps, recovering a hard mask at convergence.

3.2Sequential baseline

The sequential baseline follows standard practice: we prune to the target sparsity using magnitude criteria with layer-wise calibration, then quantize the surviving weights to 4 bits using GPTQ with 128 calibration sequences. This is a deliberately strong baseline — it is the configuration most production pipelines converge on.

4Experimental setup

We evaluate on the Pythia scaling suite at 70M, 160M, 410M, 1.0B, 1.4B, 2.8B and 6.9B parameters. Calibration uses 256 sequences of length 2048 drawn from the Pile validation split. We report zero-shot accuracy averaged over ARC-e, HellaSwag, PIQA and WinoGrande, and perplexity on WikiText-103. All runs use 50% unstructured sparsity and 4-bit weight-only quantization.

5Results

5.1Aggregate

Averaged over all scales, joint compression improves mean zero-shot accuracy by 0.8 points over the sequential baseline at 50% sparsity and 4-bit weights. The aggregate figure conceals a strong dependence on scale, which we examine next.

5.2Scale dependence

The benefit of joint optimization is monotonically decreasing in model scale. It is largest at 70M (+2.3 points) and crosses zero between 1.4B and 2.8B parameters, after which the sequential baseline is marginally better. We interpret this as evidence that larger models carry enough redundancy to absorb the approximation error introduced by treating pruning and quantization as independent stages.

3

6Limitations

Our results cover a single model family and a single sparsity target. We did not evaluate structured sparsity, which interacts differently with hardware kernels, and our quantization is weight-only; activation quantization may shift the crossover point. Finally, calibration set size was held fixed at 256 sequences, and joint optimization may be more sensitive to this choice than the sequential baseline.

7Conclusion

Joint pruning-aware quantization is worth its complexity below roughly one billion parameters and not above it. For teams serving models in the multi-billion range, the conventional sequential pipeline remains the better engineering trade.
